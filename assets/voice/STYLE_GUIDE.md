# San Velocita Broadcast Voices Style Guide

*Creative direction for character, voice, delivery, and original dialogue.*

## The broadcast we want to hear

Imagine an American motorsports television broadcast on a Sunday afternoon around 2005–2012. The grandstands are full, the cars are coming around, and two men who have spent their lives around racing settle into a conversation they have been having for years.

One is a seasoned broadcaster: warm, articulate, composed, and capable of making a great finish feel enormous. The other is a retired stock car driver: Southern, slightly raspy, mischievous, and occasionally so delighted by what he sees that he forgets to keep his voice down.

They know the sport. They respect the drivers. They like each other. They are enjoying the afternoon.

**Authenticity first, personality second, humor third.** The laughter belongs inside a believable racing broadcast. Their friendship should be audible without becoming the subject of every conversation.

Ordinary laps feel comfortable. A developing battle draws their attention. A spectacular finish pulls them out of their chairs. The excitement has weight because they have not spent the entire afternoon shouting.

The essential impression is **two real people watching the same race together**.

## The pairing at a glance

| Quality | Play-by-Play Announcer | Color Commentator |

| Background | Experienced motorsports journalist and broadcaster | Retired stock car driver |
| Apparent age | About 45–55 | About 55–65 |
| Vocal texture | Warm, rich, clear, medium-low | Warm, slightly raspy, bright midrange, natural nasal twang |
| Accent | Neutral American with subtle Midwestern warmth | Distinctive, natural North Carolina Southern drawl |
| Cadence | Smooth, rhythmic, deliberate | Looser, elastic, conversational |
| Personality | Confident, friendly, observant, dryly funny | Enthusiastic, perceptive, mischievous, occasionally goofy |

| Relationship | Enjoys his partner's unpredictability | Enjoys nudging his partner out of his composure |

Use the speaker names **Announcer** and **Color Commentator** throughout this guide. Their individual voices should be recognizable even without those labels.

## The Play-by-Play Announcer

### Who he is

He has spent decades covering American motorsports. He knows how to welcome an audience, make a complicated battle easy to follow, and recognize when a moment deserves a little space.

His professionalism is comfortable. He does not sound as though he is trying to prove that he belongs in the booth. He is intelligent and authoritative, but approachable enough that the audience enjoys spending an afternoon with him.

He appreciates skill, patience, resilience, and competitive nerve. His enthusiasm comes from his affection for racing. He can admire a bold move and still wonder whether it was wise.

The broad tradition is the warmth and clarity associated with broadcasters such as Mike Joy, Allen Bestwick, and Bob Jenkins. Take those qualities as an atmosphere; give this man his own phrasing and personality.

### Vocal texture and accent

His voice sits in a comfortable medium-low register with rounded warmth and clear consonants. It has enough body to carry a big race call without becoming booming or theatrical.

His accent is neutral American. A trace of Midwestern warmth can soften a greeting or an amused observation, but he does not adopt his partner's Southern drawl. His smile is often audible before he laughs.

At rest, he sounds like someone who can speak clearly without raising his voice. At his most excited, his pitch lifts and his delivery becomes more urgent, while the words remain intelligible.

### Cadence

He speaks in clear thought groups. He sets up an observation, gives the important detail, and lands the sentence cleanly.

> “He's closing that gap in a hurry. And the leader knows it.”

He can stretch a phrase to build anticipation, then shorten his sentences as the action tightens.

> “They come off the final corner... side by side. Here they come!”

His rhythm should feel familiar from American sports television: assured, conversational, and capable of gathering momentum. Avoid a constant ceremonial announcer voice. He can simply say, “Boy, that was a nice move,” and mean it.

### Personality and humor

He is the steadier presence, but he is not stiff. He enjoys his partner's jokes and occasionally supplies a dry punch line of his own.

His humor often lives in a restrained choice of words:

> “Well, that was certainly an ambitious move.”

> “Not quite the afternoon they were hoping for.”

> “You certainly can't accuse him of being shy.”

He does not announce that he is joking. A slight smile, a pause, or a gently raised eyebrow in the voice does the work. Sometimes his best response is a small laugh and a return to the race.

### Emotional range

- **Relaxed:** Warm and observant. He sounds content to let a conversation breathe.
- **Interested:** More focused, slightly quicker, with curiosity in the question or observation.
- **Impressed:** Open admiration, with emphasis on the detail that caught his attention.
- **Surprised:** A sharp first reaction, followed by clear speech as he finds his footing.
- **Concerned:** Firm, direct, and serious. The smile disappears; urgency remains controlled.
- **Disappointed:** Sympathetic and measured. He recognizes a driver's difficult afternoon without turning it into a melodrama.
- **Thrilled:** Faster, brighter, and forceful. Short phrases carry the call.
- **Reflective:** Settles back into warmth, giving the achievement room to mean something.

His biggest finish call should feel like composure giving way to earned excitement. It need not be his longest line.

### Language that belongs to him

“He's closing in.” “Boy, that was a terrific move.” “Here they come.” “A lot of patience there.” “What a tremendous drive.” “That's going to make things interesting.”

These illustrate his vocabulary. His identity comes from clarity, judgment, and rhythm, rather than a repeated opening phrase.

### Keep him believable

Avoid a movie-trailer growl, constant shouting, elaborate speeches during fast action, relentless superlatives, or a joke attached to every observation. His confidence should feel hospitable, never smug. He can be surprised. He can be amused. He can admit that his partner spotted something first.

## The Color Commentator

### Who he is

He has won races, lost races, worn out tires, missed braking points, and spent plenty of time explaining himself to crew chiefs. He carries that history lightly. A small, specific observation tells us more about his experience than a speech about his achievements.

Now he is in the booth, and he loves it. He gets to watch other people attempt the things he used to attempt, and he still feels those moments in his hands and stomach.

**He should sound like a race car driver who happens to have a microphone.** His sentences feel spoken in the moment, with a little roughness and a lot of personality.

The broad tradition is the warmth, enthusiasm, and driver insight associated with Darrell Waltrip and Benny Parsons. Preserve that human quality while giving him an original voice and avoiding borrowed signature catchphrases.

### Vocal texture and accent

His voice is warm, slightly raspy, and expressive, with a brighter midrange than the Announcer. A natural nasal twang gives the sound character without becoming pinched. The rasp is a lived-in edge, not a constant gravelly growl.

He has a distinctive North Carolina Southern drawl. Vowels stretch when he is comfortable or teasing. Certain words get a little upward curl. When surprised, he may climb sharply in pitch before settling back into his usual register.

The accent remains part of his voice when he becomes serious. Seriousness makes his sentences more direct; it does not turn him into a different person.

Write mostly standard spelling. A little “ya,” “gonna,” “ain't,” “gettin',” or “fixin'” can give a line flavor. Let pronunciation and rhythm carry most of the accent. Dense phonetic spelling makes him harder to read and pushes him toward caricature.

### Cadence

His rhythm is elastic. He can draw out the start of a thought, turn halfway through it, and land hard on the word that matters.

> “Now, see, he waited... waited... and then he got the run he wanted.”

He sometimes starts with the feeling before giving the explanation:

> “Aw, buddy, that's a handful. You can see him working that steering wheel.”

In calm conversation, he has an unhurried ease. In a close battle, short bursts interrupt that ease:

> “He's got a run here... oh! He's going for it!”

His spontaneity should remain easy to follow. He can abandon a thought because something has caught his eye; he should not wander through several unrelated thoughts in one sentence.

### Personality and expertise

He is quick-witted, generous, a little mischievous, and deeply knowledgeable. He respects courage, but he knows how quickly courage can become impatience.

His driver's perspective should sound physical and immediate: waiting for the car to settle, feeling the rear start to slide, wanting to press the throttle before the tires are ready. Keep these observations concise and conversational, as character dialogue rather than a lesson.

> “He's having to wait on that car. And when you're trying to catch somebody, waiting is the hardest thing in the world.”

He is allowed to be thoughtful without making a joke. He is allowed to admire restraint. He is allowed to change his mind after seeing a move work.

### Humor

He reacts to what is happening with a colorful image or a knowing aside. He rarely sounds as though he arrived with a prepared punch line.

> “Well... that's one way to get a better look at the scenery.”

> “I don't believe that's the fastest way around this racetrack.”

> “I've been in that situation. It ain't nearly as much fun as it looks from up here.”

His best jokes have an affectionate quality. He can laugh at an overambitious move because he recognizes himself in it. He can tease the Announcer's careful wording because he admires him.

Avoid stacking several folksy comparisons into one line. One good image is enough. Avoid turning every mistake into an insult, or treating Southern speech as a joke in itself.

### Emotional range

- **Relaxed:** Easy drawl, warm curiosity, conversational pauses.
- **Thoughtful:** A little quieter and more direct; experience gives the words weight.
- **Playful:** A smile in the voice, a stretched word, perhaps a brief chuckle.
- **Impressed:** Brightens with admiration, often noticing something the driver did well.
- **Incredulous:** Pitch rises; he sounds caught off guard and eager to share it.
- **Excited:** Sudden, compact bursts of delight. The emotion can briefly outrun his sentence.
- **Concerned:** The banter falls away. His voice becomes sober and empathetic.
- **Relieved:** Releases tension with an exhale, a softer phrase, or a small laugh once the danger has passed.
- **Moved:** Warm, plainspoken pride. He does not need a joke to finish the thought.

He is usually a little more emotionally exposed than the Announcer. That difference should be pleasurable to hear, not exhausting.

### Language that belongs to him

“Aw, buddy.” “Man alive.” “Now, see, here's the thing.” “That'll get your attention.” “I've been there.” “He made that thing stick.” “Boy, that's a handful.”

These are a palette, not a mandatory refrain. Many of his strongest lines should begin without a catchphrase.

### Keep him believable

Avoid a cartoon cowboy, an exaggerated parody of a familiar broadcaster, nonstop hollering, forced slang, or a lovable fool who never contributes insight. His experience is central to his charm. His enthusiasm has substance behind it.

## Their chemistry

### Longtime colleagues who enjoy each other

They do not need to explain their friendship. We hear it in the ease of a question, the speed of a response, and the confidence that a little teasing will be understood.

The Announcer gives the conversation shape. The Color Commentator brings a driver's eye and an unpredictable emotional turn. Neither is trapped in a single conversational job: the Announcer can be funny, and the Color Commentator can make a calm, precise observation.

They respect each other's strengths. The Color Commentator does not ridicule the Announcer for never having driven professionally. The Announcer does not treat his partner as comic relief.

### Respond to the thought, not just the topic

A believable reply picks up something the other man actually said. It might echo one word, answer a question, challenge a judgment, or extend an observation.

> **Announcer:** “A very patient move.”
>
> **Color Commentator:** “Patient right up until he wasn't! He saw that opening and took it.”

That small connection makes the exchange feel shared. Two separate descriptions of the same pass do less for their relationship.

### Friendly disagreement

They can disagree about a driver's choice without turning the booth into an argument.

> **Announcer:** “A little too ambitious, perhaps?”
>
> **Color Commentator:** “Maybe. But from where he was sitting, that gap probably looked awfully inviting.”

Let each perspective have merit. Sometimes the disagreement ends with a laugh; sometimes the race gives them something else to watch.

### Give each other space

They do not both need the longest line or the loudest reaction. A short “Oh, buddy!” can sit beside a clear race call. An insightful observation can stand on its own without a joke afterward.

Interruptions should feel affectionate or involuntary: a burst of surprise when something changes in front of them. Neither man habitually talks over the other to claim attention.

A natural conversation can end after two lines. Do not force every exchange into setup, punch line, laugh, and closing remark.

## Writing original dialogue

### Write for the mouth and ear

Favor contractions, concrete verbs, and sentences a person can say comfortably in one breath. Read a line aloud and listen for where it wants to pause.

Too formal:

> “The competitor has executed an exceptionally impressive overtaking maneuver on the interior portion of the circuit.”

Natural Announcer:

> “A terrific run off the corner. Right down the inside!”

Natural Color Commentator:

> “He set that up beautifully. Had the run before the other fella knew he was coming.”

### Let sentence length follow the feeling

Calm conversation can carry a complete thought. Tension tightens the phrasing. Surprise may arrive as a fragment. Reflection after a finish can slow down again.

Do not mistake short dialogue for empty dialogue. “He waited for that” can carry admiration, insight, and personality when it is the right response.

### Use racing language naturally

Phrases such as “getting a run,” “protecting the inside,” “carrying momentum,” “getting loose,” “running out of grip,” and “making the pass stick” belong in their speech. They should arise from what the men are discussing rather than decorate every sentence.

Let the vocabulary fit the kind of racing being described. Their voices remain the same across stock cars, open-wheel racing, and other disciplines; the conversation should still sound at home in the sport in front of them.

### Keep the emotional contrast

The Announcer is usually the clearer, steadier voice. The Color Commentator is usually the more elastic, personal voice. A rewrite should preserve those differences even when both men share the same emotion.

In admiration, the Announcer might say, “A tremendous piece of driving.” His partner might say, “I don't know how he hung on to that thing.” Both are impressed; they express it differently.

### Let humor breathe

Use a joke when the observation invites it. Leave sincere admiration sincere. Let an anecdote be brief enough that we are still watching the race with them.

A harmless mistake can invite a smile. A serious accident brings concern and restraint. Neither man turns distress into entertainment or casually assumes someone is all right.

### Write fresh thoughts, not shuffled catchphrases

Vary the first word, sentence shape, image, and emotional landing. A collection of lines that all begin “Well, I'll tell ya what” will flatten the Color Commentator. A collection that all end “Unbelievable!” will flatten the Announcer.

Build new dialogue from each man's point of view: what he notices, what he cares about, and what he wants to say to his partner. The examples in this guide establish character and tone; they are not templates to repeat verbatim.

## ElevenLabs delivery-tag examples

The bracketed phrases below are **creative performance direction**: the feeling, texture, and rhythm to imagine while reading the line.

### Announcer: warmth through excitement

> [warm, confident, conversational] “Good afternoon, race fans. We've got a beautiful day and a terrific field ready to go.”

> [observant, relaxed, lightly intrigued] “He's been quietly working his way forward. Now he's right in the middle of this.”

> [dryly amused, with a small smile] “Well, that certainly got everybody's attention.”

> [focused, gradually building anticipation] “The gap is coming down... and he's almost close enough to have a look.”

> [sudden surprise, urgent but clear] “Oh, trouble in turn three! Hard into the wall.”

> [intensely excited, accelerating, clear emphasis] “Side by side! Coming to the line! He gets him!”

> [warm, reflective, sincerely impressed] “That was a remarkable drive. He had to work for every bit of it.”

### Color Commentator: ease through exhilaration

> [relaxed Southern drawl, thoughtful] “He's taking his time with it. Sometimes that's the hardest part.”

> [playful, a smile in the voice] “You make that sound a whole lot easier than it feels from the driver's seat.”

> [matter-of-fact, quietly experienced] “He's having to wait before he can get back to the throttle. That car just isn't settled yet.”

> [dryly amused, brief chuckle] “Well... I don't believe he meant to take the scenic route.”

> [astonished, rising in pitch, delighted] “Oh, buddy! How did he keep that thing straight?!”

> [sober, concerned, direct] “That's a hard hit. Let's hope we see him get out of there.”

> [softened, relieved, warm] “There he is. Boy, that's good to see.”

### A feeling can change inside a line

> **Announcer:** [conversational, attentive] “He's getting closer off the corner...” [suddenly animated] “And now he's alongside!”

> **Color Commentator:** [relaxed, admiring] “He's got a good run here...” [surprised, delighted, rising in pitch] “OH! He's gonna try it!”

Keep the direction focused on the meaningful change. A short line usually needs one clear emotional intention. Avoid a pile of competing adjectives that makes the performance hard to imagine.

Ellipses suggest a pause or a thought taking shape. Selective capitals suggest a stressed word. Exclamation marks suggest energy. Use these sparingly enough that ordinary speech still looks and sounds ordinary.

Excitement changes pitch, pace, and force. It does not erase the Announcer's clarity or the Color Commentator's drawl.

## Sample exchanges

### The original opening: their defining chemistry

**Announcer:** [warm, professional, building anticipation] “Well, we've got a packed grandstand, a beautiful afternoon, and thirty-two drivers who all think they can win this thing!”

**Color Commentator:** [playful, natural Southern drawl, chuckling] “Well, I'll tell ya what, buddy... thirty-two of 'em might THINK they can win, but about twenty-nine of 'em are fixin' to find out otherwise!”

**Announcer:** [laughing warmly, amused] “Well, that's one way to look at it!”

**Color Commentator:** [suddenly animated, rising in pitch] “Aw, come on now! You know I'm right! Let's get these boys RACIN'!”

The charm is the contrast: a confident welcome, a mischievous interruption, a warm laugh, and enthusiasm they share.

### An easy afternoon in the booth

**Announcer:** [relaxed, conversational] “A little breathing room for the leader now.”

**Color Commentator:** [easy drawl, knowing] “Little bit. I never trusted breathing room until I could see the checkered flag.”

**Announcer:** [lightly amused] “You weren't much for relaxing, were you?”

**Color Commentator:** [chuckling, matter-of-fact] “Not while somebody behind me was having a better afternoon.”

### A clean, patient pass

**Announcer:** [impressed, controlled] “A beautiful run off the corner. He's through.”

**Color Commentator:** [warm, appreciative] “That's patience right there. He waited for a run he could do something with.”

### An ambitious move

**Announcer:** [surprised, animated] “Oh, he's going for it! Way down on the inside!”

**Color Commentator:** [explosive surprise, incredulous] “OH, BUDDY! He just threw that thing in there!”

**Announcer:** [excited, impressed] “And somehow, he makes it stick!”

**Color Commentator:** [laughing briefly, admiring] “I don't know how he did it... but I like it!”

### Insight without a lecture

**Announcer:** [observant, conversational] “He's really having to work that car off the corners.”

**Color Commentator:** [thoughtful, experienced] “Yeah, he's waiting on it to settle down. And every second he waits feels about ten seconds long when he's chasing somebody.”

**Announcer:** [engaged, warm] “A tough place to be when you can see the car you're trying to catch.”

### A harmless mistake

**Announcer:** [surprised, then composed] “Oh, he's missed the corner completely. Back on track now.”

**Color Commentator:** [dryly amused, small chuckle] “Well... that's one way to get a better look at the scenery.”

**Announcer:** [briefly amused, settling back] “Unfortunately, it's cost him a couple of spots.”

### Affectionate disagreement

**Announcer:** [considering, mildly doubtful] “I wonder if he might have been better off waiting another corner.”

**Color Commentator:** [friendly, animated] “Maybe. But when you see a little daylight, it's awful hard to tell your right foot to be patient.”

**Announcer:** [dryly amused] “That sounds like experience talking.”

**Color Commentator:** [chuckling, self-deprecating] “Experience and a couple of unhappy crew chiefs.”

### A brief driver's memory

**Announcer:** [curious, relaxed] “How much do you think about the car behind when the gap gets that small?”

**Color Commentator:** [knowing, unhurried drawl] “More than you ought to. I used to look in that mirror so much I nearly forgot there was a corner in front of me.”

**Announcer:** [smiling, gently teasing] “Nearly?”

**Color Commentator:** [brief laugh] “We're gonna leave it at nearly.”

### A spectacular save

**Announcer:** [sharply surprised, urgent] “Oh, the rear steps out! He's sideways!”

**Color Commentator:** [tense, quick bursts] “Hang on... hang on...”

**Announcer:** [relieved, impressed] “And he gathers it up. What a save!”

**Color Commentator:** [astonished, releasing a small laugh] “Man alive. I'd have needed a minute after that.”

### A serious accident

**Announcer:** [shocked, clear, genuinely concerned] “Hard into the wall. That is a heavy impact.”

**Color Commentator:** [sober, direct] “It certainly is. Let's hope we see him climb out.”

Their restraint carries the moment. Concern should be audible without speculation or dramatic embellishment.

### Relief afterward

**Announcer:** [relieved, warm] “And the driver is out of the car.”

**Color Commentator:** [softened, sincere] “Boy, that's good to see.”

Let this exchange remain simple. It does not need a punch line.

### A finish that pulls them out of their seats

**Announcer:** [intensely excited, rapidly building energy] “Here they come! Side by side, heading for the checkered flag!”

**Color Commentator:** [burst of exhilaration, rising in pitch] “COME ON, BABY! Who's it gonna be?!”

**Announcer:** [explosive, triumphant, clear] “And he gets him at the line! Unbelievable!”

**Color Commentator:** [astonished, laughing with excitement] “Oh, man! That's racin' right there!”

### A quiet moment after a hard-earned win

**Announcer:** [warm, reflective] “There were plenty of times today when that win looked a long way off.”

**Color Commentator:** [sincere, quietly proud] “He stayed with it. That's what I love about that drive. He just kept giving himself another chance.”

### The Announcer gets the laugh

**Color Commentator:** [confident, amused] “I would've tried that pass.”

**Announcer:** [dry, a small smile] “Yes. That's what concerns me.”

**Color Commentator:** [surprised chuckle, affectionate] “Aw, you're not gonna let me have that one, are ya?”

## The creative standard

When writing new lines, listen for these qualities:

- Each man has a distinct voice, rhythm, and point of view.
- The dialogue sounds comfortable when spoken aloud.
- Replies connect to something the other man actually said.
- Excitement grows out of what they are watching.
- The Color Commentator contributes insight as readily as humor.
- The Announcer has warmth and personality beneath his composure.
- The Southern accent feels natural, with restraint in the spelling.
- Humor is affectionate, brief, and suited to the moment.
- Serious moments retain empathy and dignity.
- A sincere thought can stand without a joke or a catchphrase.

Above all, write two men who love racing and enjoy watching it together. Their character should live in what they notice, how they say it, and how they respond to one another.
